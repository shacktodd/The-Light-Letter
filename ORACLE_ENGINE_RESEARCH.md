## 01 — Mosque timekeeping was an applied-astronomy institution [E]

**Claim.** From at least the thirteenth century, prominent urban mosques employed a *muwaqqit*: an astronomy specialist who fixed prayer times and religious calendars. The office connected instruments, astronomical tables, and collective ritual coordination.[1] [2]

**Inversion.** The modern default separates devotion from exact science. The documented role shows that recurrent demands of prayer time, qiblah, and lunar months made astronomical calculation operational, not merely decorative.

**How we know.** HMML documents mosque-employed timekeepers, quadrants, astrolabes, practical treatises, calendrical tables, and identifies Ibn al-Shāṭir (1304–1375) as *muwaqqit* at Damascus’s Umayyad Mosque.[1] King’s specialist study locates Mamluk astronomy and the office in a broader scholarly record.[2]

**Falsifier.** A representative prosopography showing that mosque *muwaqqits* rarely performed astronomical calculation, or that the cited instruments and tables were not used for timekeeping, would break this institutional claim.

**Quarantine.** The office, duties, practitioners, instruments, and manuscripts are documented. It is interpreted—not documented—that mosque patrons aimed to advance science or that religious demand alone explains innovation.

**Steelman and audit.** The connection is load-bearing because the office needed repeatable solar/lunar calculation and supplied an institutional home for it. The major gap is sampling: surviving elite urban manuscripts overrepresent instrument-rich centers. The denominator excludes small mosques, informal timekeepers, lost tables, and users who relied on schedules without instruments. The intentionality failure would be to infer a patron-designed innovation policy from a technical service role.

**So what.** The productive question is not “did religion allow science?” but “which routine institutions turned precision into collective service?”

## 02 — Imperial paperwork accidentally became a climate archive [E]

**Claim.** Naval logs from British, Dutch, French, and Spanish vessels engaged in imperial business became usable pre-instrumental climate data. The CLIWOC project abstracted more than 280,000 daily weather observations for 1750–1854 and used them for reconstructions relevant to oceanic wind fields, ENSO, and the NAO.[3] NOAA’s ICOADS documents a provenance-aware surface-marine collection spanning 1662 to the present.[4]

**Inversion.** The intuitive story makes purpose-built instruments the beginning of climate knowledge. Here, routine navigation-and-command paperwork became a distributed environmental sensor after the fact.

**How we know.** García-Herrera and colleagues give the observation count, national archives, period, imperial setting, and reconstruction use; they also report that the first coordinated database represented less than ten percent of the then-known archival volume.[3] NOAA documents the collection, platform types, products, and source-provenance practices.[4]

**Falsifier.** Independent transcription and calibration audits showing that the weather fields were systematically copied, non-contemporaneous, or could not retain agreement with independent climate indicators would defeat the “usable record” claim.

**Quarantine.** The later scientific reuse is documented. “Accidentally” means climate reconstruction was not the original objective; it does not assign a single purpose to imperial administration or neutralize the violence embedded in imperial expansion.

**Steelman and audit.** The passage from log field to standardized, geolocated, quality-checked observation is real, not metaphorical. But coverage follows imperial routes and the known archive is far larger than the digitized subset. The denominator includes voyages never logged or preserved, non-European mariners, and unstrategic routes. The intentionality audit blocks the claim that the original bureaucracy “meant” to observe global climate.

**So what.** Historical harm and scientific value can inhabit the same record. Provenance belongs in the analysis, not in a footnote.

## 03 — A census category can become a political audience [C]

**Claim.** In late colonial India, official attempts to describe and classify social complexity helped stimulate caste-cluster consciousness and organizations. A public category could be mobilized around, contested, and revised rather than merely received.[5]

**Inversion.** A census appears to discover a population already there. The more careful proposition is that public classification can become an argumentative target without inventing every identity from nothing.

**How we know.** Carroll’s historical analysis argues that caste organizations and polemics responded substantially to foreign efforts to define and categorize Indian society, calling the effect largely unintended. The work distinguishes caste-clusters from single endogamous *jatis* and cites actors directly disputing ethnological accounts.[5]

**Falsifier.** A multi-region design showing no rise in category-based organizations, petitions, or claims after comparable classification exposure—or showing that classification adds no explanatory value after communication, law, education, local hierarchy, and resources are measured—would weaken the feedback account.

**Quarantine.** Enumerative practice, category disputes, associations, and printed argument are documented. Neither a colonial state intention to manufacture caste politics nor the erasure of older social forms and Indian agency follows from that evidence.

**Steelman and audit.** Cases contain direct feedback evidence: actors argued over ethnological descriptions and organized under public category names that crossed older endogamous boundaries. Yet sampling concentrates literate elites, visible associations, regional cases, and successful organizations. “Caste” itself is semantically hazardous, potentially flattening *jati*, caste-cluster, *varna*, occupation, and political association. The major open problem is a credible national causal decomposition of enumeration, law, print, rail, education, local hierarchy, and indigenous strategy.

**So what.** The relevant governance question is not whether a label is “merely data,” but whether it is public, repeatable, contestable, and attached to claimable stakes.

## Gap-derived hypothesis [S]

> **Conditional configuration hypothesis.** A public classification is most likely to become an organizing identity when it is paired with a contestable resource—representation, status, law, or allocation—not merely when a state prints the label.

This is more parsimonious than “classification creates identity” because it predicts variation. It should be tested with a province-by-category panel that links classification exposure, resource-linked rules, association and petition formation, print reach, and baseline hierarchy. It fails if resource linkage adds no predictive power, or if classification has the same effect where no resource channel exists.

The hypothesis passes the denominator check only if non-associations and missing archives are counted, and it passes the intentionality check only because it does not require a state plan to manufacture identity. It remains **[S]**, not a conclusion.
