# Recovery Edition — Source Notes

## Nilometer records

F. A. Hassan’s *Science* article, “Historical Nile Floods and Their Implications for Climatic Change,” reports analysis of Nile flood stages from **A.D. 640 to 1921**. Its abstract identifies low-discharge intervals (930–1070 and 1180–1350), high-flood intervals (1070–1180 and 1350–1470), and comparisons with Lake Chad water levels. The source supports a narrow claim: historic flood-stage records can inform regional hydroclimate reconstruction. It does **not** independently prove motives, political consequences, or direct causation from climate to social change.

## Kyoto flowering records

The intended peer-reviewed source is Aono & Kazui, “Phenological data series of cherry tree flowering in Kyoto, Japan, and its application to reconstruction of springtime temperatures since the 9th century” (*International Journal of Climatology*, DOI: 10.1002/joc.1594). The publisher page currently presents an access challenge, so the edition will use a direct DOI and state the inference conservatively: cultural-event records can become a temperature proxy only after explicit calibration and uncertainty treatment.

## Current relevance

NOAA’s July 2026 national assessment reports the contiguous United States’ warmest July in the 1895–present record. Berkeley Earth’s July 2026 update reports the second-warmest July globally in its series. These provide dated current-event context for an edition about how long records, including non-instrumental ones, shape climate interpretation. The connection is methodological, not evidence that any particular historical record predicts present-day temperature.
